"3A" or "6A" on a cable is its current rating: the most electrical current, in amps, the cable is built to carry. It is a ceiling, not a promise. Your phone and charger decide how much current actually flows, and a cable rated higher than that simply is not the bottleneck.

# Amps, volts and watts in one minute

Charging power is measured in watts. The sum is the same one printed on every charger: **volts × amps = watts.**

- 5V × 2A = 10W, standard charging.
- 9V × 2A = 18W, a common fast-charging level.
- 5V × 3A = 15W.
- 20V × 3A = 60W.

So the amp rating on a cable tells you how much current it can carry, and the volts come from the charger. A cable rated 3A can carry up to 60W at 20V; at 5V the same 3A is only 15W.

# What the rating does

Current flowing through a wire creates heat. Thicker copper and better connectors carry more current with less heat and less voltage loss. The rating is the maker's statement of how much current the cable is designed to handle.

- **A higher rating gives headroom.** If your charger and phone want to use 3A, a 3A cable will not hold them back.
- **A higher rating does not force more power.** A 6A cable on a charger that gives 2A still carries 2A. The phone takes only what it can handle.
- **A rating below what you need slows you down.** A thin cable on a fast charger may make the phone charge at a lower speed.

# When 3A is enough, and when 6A matters

For most phones on most chargers, a well-made **3A cable** is enough. That covers standard charging and many fast-charging setups.

Some phone makers use their own fast-charging systems that push higher current through the cable, often over USB-A to USB-C. Those systems need a cable rated for that current, and they need the matching charger and phone too. A **6A cable** is built for that kind of load and gives extra headroom for everyday use.

For USB-C to USB-C fast charging with USB Power Delivery (PD), cables are usually described in watts instead, for example 20W, 60W or 100W. Most USB-C cables carry up to 60W; for 100W you need a cable rated for 100W (5A).

# 3A does not mean USB 3.0

This is a common mix-up. **Amps describe electrical current. USB 3.0 is a data speed.** A cable marked 3A does not, by itself, transfer data at USB 3.0 speed. If you need fast file transfer to a computer, check the cable's data specification separately.

# Reading a listing

When you compare cables, look for:

- **The connectors** at both ends.
- **The rating,** in amps or watts.
- **The length.** A longer cable needs good copper to keep its rating useful.
- **The build:** braided or plain, and whether the plugs are reinforced.

> If a cable gets hot at the plug while charging, stop using it, whatever its rating says. Heat there usually means damage or a poor connection.
