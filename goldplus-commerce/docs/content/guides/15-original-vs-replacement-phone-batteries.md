An original battery is the one the phone maker fitted, or sells as a spare part under its own name. A replacement battery is made by another supplier to fit the same phone. A good replacement matches the original's code, voltage, connector and size, and does the same job. What differs is who made it, how its capacity is stated, and, on some phones, how the phone recognises it.

Here is what to look at, without the marketing.

# What must be the same

For a replacement to be safe and to work properly, these have to match the original:

- **The battery code**, such as BL-49FT, or for iPhones, the exact model it is made for.
- **The nominal voltage**, for example 3.87V.
- **The charge limit voltage**, if printed, for example 4.4V.
- **The connector and the size**, so it fits without force and connects properly.

If any of these differ, it is the wrong battery, whatever the label says about quality.

# What can differ

## How capacity is stated

Some labels print a rated and a typical capacity. Others print one figure. Compare like with like. A higher mAh number on a replacement is not automatically better: what matters is that it is the right battery for the phone.

## The protection circuit

Every lithium phone battery has a small protection board that guards against overcharging, deep discharge and short circuits. Its design can differ from maker to maker. You cannot see this from outside, which is why buying from a seller who stands behind the product matters.

## How the phone reads it

Phones track the battery's charge with a small chip and software. A new battery, original or replacement, needs a few charge cycles before the percentage settles.

On many recent iPhones, iOS checks whether a battery is a genuine Apple part fitted through Apple's repair process. If it cannot confirm that, Settings may show a notice that the battery cannot be verified, and Battery Health may show less detail. The phone still works and charges.

## Who made the cells

The cells inside any battery come from a cell manufacturer, which may or may not be the same company whose name is on the label. That is true of original and replacement batteries alike.

# What does not make a battery better

- **A big number on the front.** The code and the voltage decide fit; a larger capacity on a battery made for the same phone should be treated with caution until you can compare like with like.
- **Shiny packaging.** It tells you about the packaging.
- **Price alone.** A very cheap battery may be fine or may not; you cannot tell from the price.

# How to spot a fake in general

- The label is blurry, misspelt or peels off easily.
- The code is missing or does not match any known battery.
- The battery feels unusually light or looks puffy before use.
- There is no seller you can go back to if something goes wrong.

# What to check before you buy

1. Your exact model number, from Settings, then About phone.
2. The code on your old battery, if you can see it without opening a sealed phone.
3. That the replacement's code and voltage match.
4. That the seller confirms the fit and has a clear [returns](/returns) and [warranty](/warranty) policy.
