A microSD card label tells you three things: **how much it holds**, **what card type it is**, and **the minimum speed it is rated to write**. For most phones, a Class 10 card in the right size for your needs is enough. For 4K video, dashcams and CCTV, the speed markings matter more.

# Size and card type

The card type is tied to the size:

- **microSD:** up to 2GB. Used in old phones, radios and MP3 players.
- **microSDHC** (High Capacity): more than 2GB, up to 32GB.
- **microSDXC** (Extended Capacity): more than 32GB, up to 2TB.

This matters because an older device may only read up to a certain type. A phone or radio that supports microSDHC but not microSDXC will not read a 64GB card. Check your device's manual or settings for the largest card it supports before buying a big one.

# Speed class markings

Speed markings show the **minimum steady write speed**, which is what matters when a camera or phone records video without stopping.

- **Speed Class (a number in a C):** Class 2, 4, 6 and 10 mean at least 2, 4, 6 and 10 MB per second. Class 10 is the usual choice for phones.
- **UHS Speed Class (a number in a U):** U1 means at least 10 MB per second; U3 means at least 30 MB per second.
- **Video Speed Class (V followed by a number):** V6, V10, V30, V60 and V90 mean at least that many MB per second. V30 is commonly recommended for 4K video.
- **App Performance Class (A1 or A2):** about how quickly the card handles the small reads and writes of apps running from it.

A card may also print a "read speed up to" figure. That is a maximum, usually much higher than the write speed, and it is not the same as the class.

# What do you need?

- **Phone photos, music and documents:** Class 10 is plenty.
- **Full HD phone video:** Class 10 or U1.
- **4K video, action cameras and drones:** check the camera's manual; many ask for U3 or V30.
- **CCTV and dashcams:** these write non-stop, so follow the recorder's recommended class and size.

# Why your phone shows a little less

Card makers count 1GB as 1,000,000,000 bytes. Phones and computers often count in units of 1,024, so the same card shows a smaller number on screen. A 64GB card may show about 59GB. The system also keeps a small part for its file table. This is normal and is not missing space.

What is not normal is a card that claims a size it does not have. That is capacity fraud, and the warning signs are files that will not open or that vanish. Every memory card we sell is written full and read back before it is sold, so the size on the box is the size you get.
