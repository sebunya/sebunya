USB Power Delivery, usually shortened to PD, is a fast-charging standard that works over USB-C. A PD charger and a PD device talk to each other first, agree on a voltage, and only then send more power. If they cannot agree, the charger falls back to plain 5V and the device charges at normal speed.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this guide explains the details in plain words.

# Why a higher voltage charges faster

Power in watts is volts multiplied by amps. An ordinary USB charger gives 5 volts. To send more power at 5V, the current (amps) has to go up, and more current means more heat in the cable and the connectors.

PD solves this by raising the voltage instead. At 9V, the same current carries almost twice as much power as at 5V. That is why a PD charger's label lists several lines, such as 5V, 9V, 12V or more, each with its own current.

# The handshake: nothing happens until both sides agree

When you plug in a PD device, three things happen in a moment:

1. The charger starts at a safe 5V.
2. It tells the device which voltage and current profiles it can supply.
3. The device picks the profile it wants, and the charger switches to it.

This is why PD is safe to use with many different devices. A phone asks for what it can handle, and a charger only gives what it was asked for. A PD charger that can supply a laptop will still charge a small phone at the phone's own pace.

# What PPS adds

Some PD chargers also support **PPS** (Programmable Power Supply). Instead of jumping between fixed steps like 5V and 9V, PPS lets the device ask for small changes in voltage as it charges. Some Android phones, including Samsung's Super Fast Charging models, use PPS for their fastest mode.

If a phone wants PPS and the charger only has standard PD, the phone still charges, usually at a slower fast-charge speed. The charger's label or listing will say PPS if it supports it.

# You need USB-C at both ends of the link

PD runs over USB-C. In practice that means:

- The charger needs a **USB-C port** that supports PD.
- The cable needs **USB-C at the charger end**. The other end is USB-C for most Android phones and newer iPhones, or Lightning for iPhones that use it.
- A USB-A port cannot do PD, even on a charger that has a PD USB-C port next to it.

So if you plug a USB-C phone into the USB-A port of a dual-port charger, you are not using PD on that port.

# The cable matters more than people think

The cable is part of the conversation. A damaged cable can stop the handshake or force the charger back to 5V.

- Most USB-C to USB-C cables are built for up to 3A, which is up to 60W at 20V.
- Above 60W, PD needs 5A, and only a cable built and marked for 5A (100W or more) can carry it.
- For phones, a good USB-C to USB-C cable in good condition is enough. For a laptop, check the cable's watt rating.

# How to tell if you are fast charging

Your phone usually tells you. Many Android phones show "Fast charging" or "Super fast charging" on the lock screen. iPhones do not show a message, but you will notice the battery climbing much faster in the first half of the charge.

If it charges slowly, check three things in this order: the port (USB-C, not USB-A), the cable (USB-C at the charger end, in good condition), and the phone's own spec (does it support PD at all?).

# Choosing a PD charger

- Check that the listing or label says **PD** for the USB-C port.
- Read the output lines for that port, as explained in our guide on reading a charger's label. The highest line is the most it lists.
- Check your phone's spec for its maximum charging power and whether it needs PPS.
- Pick a cable that matches: USB-C to USB-C, or USB-C to Lightning for a Lightning iPhone.
