It matters when you move **large files**, like films, full phone backups or big folders of photos, and when your computer has a **USB 3.0 port**. For a few documents or songs, you will hardly notice. A USB 3.0 drive works in a USB 2.0 port too, just at USB 2.0 speed, so it is never a step backwards.

# The difference on paper

- **USB 2.0** has a top data rate of 480 megabits per second.
- **USB 3.0** has a top data rate of 5 gigabits per second, roughly ten times more.

You may also see USB 3.0 called **USB 3.1 Gen 1** or **USB 3.2 Gen 1**. They are the same 5 gigabit speed under newer names.

These are the limits of the connection, not what any drive actually achieves. Real speed depends on the memory inside the drive, the size of the files, and the computer. Copying one big film is usually much faster than copying thousands of small files of the same total size.

# You need both ends

To get USB 3.0 speed, **the drive and the port** both have to support it.

- USB 3.0 drive in a USB 3.0 port: USB 3.0 speed.
- USB 3.0 drive in a USB 2.0 port: works, at USB 2.0 speed.
- USB 2.0 drive in a USB 3.0 port: works, at USB 2.0 speed.

# How to spot a USB 3.0 port

- The plastic inside the port is often **blue**. This is common but not guaranteed.
- There may be an **"SS"** (SuperSpeed) logo next to the port.
- Your computer's manual or the maker's website will list the ports.

Older computers, many TVs and most car stereos have USB 2.0 ports. For playing music in a car or a film on a TV, that is fine: they only need to read the file as fast as it plays.

# When USB 3.0 is worth it

- **Backing up a phone or laptop,** where you copy many gigabytes at once.
- **Moving films and video footage,** which are large files.
- **Working from the drive,** opening and saving files on it regularly.
- **Large drives.** Filling a 128GB drive over USB 2.0 can take a long time.

# When USB 2.0 is fine

- Carrying a few documents to print at a shop in town.
- Music for the car.
- Small school or office files.
- Devices that only have USB 2.0 ports anyway.

# Other things that slow a drive down

- **Many small files.** Zip a big folder of small files into one file before copying, and it often goes faster.
- **A nearly full drive** can slow down.
- **A front-panel or hub port** on a desktop may be slower than one at the back of the computer.
- **The format.** FAT32 cannot hold a single file larger than 4GB; for big films, exFAT is the usual choice.

> Always use "Eject" or "Safely remove" before unplugging a drive, especially after copying large files. Pulling it out mid-copy can corrupt the files, and power cuts during a copy can do the same.
