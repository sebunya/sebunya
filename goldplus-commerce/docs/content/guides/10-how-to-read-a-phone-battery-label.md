The most important line on a phone battery label is the battery code, for example BL-49FT. That code is what decides whether a replacement fits. The other lines, such as mAh, voltage and watt-hours, describe the battery but do not tell you which phone it belongs in.

Here is each part of a typical label, in the order you are likely to see it.

# The battery code (model)

This is a short code, often near the top or printed largest. Examples:

- Tecno, Infinix and itel batteries usually start with **BL-**, such as BL-49FT or BL-58BX.
- Samsung batteries usually start with **EB-**.
- Other brands use their own patterns.

Copy the whole code exactly, including letters at the end. Codes that differ by one letter can be different batteries. When you buy a replacement, the code on the new battery should match the code on the old one.

# Capacity: rated and typical (mAh)

Capacity is printed in milliamp-hours (mAh). Some labels print two figures:

- **Rated (or minimum) capacity:** the lowest the maker says the battery will hold under its test conditions.
- **Typical capacity:** the average it holds under those conditions.

Both describe the same battery. When you compare two batteries, compare rated with rated and typical with typical. Neither figure tells you which phone the battery fits.

# Nominal voltage (V)

Usually written as something like 3.85V or 3.87V. This is the battery's average working voltage. A replacement should have the same nominal voltage as the original. If it does not, do not use it.

# Charge limit voltage

Some labels also print a charge limit voltage, often a little over 4V, for example 4.4V or 4.45V. This is the highest voltage the phone should charge it to. It should also match the original.

# Energy (Wh)

Watt-hours (Wh) are the true measure of how much energy the battery stores. If the label does not print it, you can work it out:

**mAh × nominal voltage ÷ 1,000 = Wh**

As a worked example only: 5,000mAh × 3.87V ÷ 1,000 = about 19.4Wh. Watt-hours matter when you fly with a spare battery, because airline rules are written in Wh.

# Battery type

Usually "Li-ion" (lithium-ion) or "Li-polymer" (lithium-ion polymer). Both are rechargeable lithium batteries and are handled the same way for safety.

# Warnings and symbols

Most labels carry some or all of these:

- **Do not puncture, crush or burn.** Taken seriously: a damaged lithium battery can catch fire.
- **Do not short-circuit.** Keep the metal contacts away from coins, keys and other metal.
- **Crossed-out bin symbol.** Do not throw it in household rubbish. Take it to a place that collects batteries.
- **Recycling and certification marks.** These vary by market.

# Dates and batch numbers

Some labels carry a date or batch code. Formats differ by maker, so do not try to decode one without knowing the maker's format.

# What to compare when buying a replacement

1. **The code.** It must match.
2. **The nominal voltage.** It should match.
3. **The capacity.** It should be close to the original, compared like with like.

If the label is worn or you cannot read it, your model number from Settings, then About phone, is enough to start. Never open a sealed phone to read a label, and never handle a swollen battery to find its code.
