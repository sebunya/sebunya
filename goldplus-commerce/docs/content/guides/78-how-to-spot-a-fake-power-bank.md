A fake power bank usually gives itself away: a huge capacity claim on a small, light body, a missing or vague label, poor build, and charging that runs out far too soon. # Why fakes matter

A fake power bank is not just poor value. It may use poor or recycled cells and skip the protection circuits that stop overheating and overcharging. That makes it more likely to get hot, swell or fail. A power bank sits in your pocket and charges next to your bed, so this matters.

# Warning sign 1: capacity that does not add up

Energy takes space and weight. A small, light power bank claiming a very large capacity, such as tens of thousands of mAh in something the size of a lighter, is a red flag. If a power bank feels much lighter than others of the same stated size, be suspicious.

Another clue is performance. If a "20,000mAh" unit struggles to fully charge an ordinary phone even once, be suspicious. All power banks give less than their label, because of conversion losses, but not that much less.

# Warning sign 2: a poor or missing label

A genuine power bank carries a clear label with:

- Capacity in mAh, and often in Wh.
- Input and output ratings in volts and amps.
- A model name or number.
- Maker details.

Missing figures, spelling mistakes, smudged or peeling print, or numbers that contradict the box are all warning signs.

# Warning sign 3: build quality

- Gaps at the seams, or a case that creaks or flexes.
- Something rattling inside.
- Loose or wobbly ports.
- Packaging that is flimsy, poorly printed or already opened.

# Warning sign 4: how it behaves

- It gets very hot during normal use.
- The charge display jumps around or drops suddenly.
- It takes forever to recharge but empties quickly.
- Your phone shows charging errors or stops and starts.

Any of these, especially heat, means stop using it.

# Warning sign 5: a price that seems too good

A price far below what similar power banks cost is a common sign. Good cells and protection circuits cost money.
