Uganda uses the UK-style three-pin plug, also called Type G: three rectangular pins in a triangle. The simplest and safest choice is a charger with a UK plug built in, so it goes straight into the socket with no adapter. Every wall charger we sell has a UK three-pin plug.

# How the Type G plug works

The plug has three pins:

- **Live and neutral:** the two lower pins carry the current.
- **Earth:** the top, longer pin. It goes in first and, on most sockets, pushes open the shutters that cover the live and neutral holes.

Those shutters are a safety feature. They stop children, or anything else, being pushed into the live holes.

# Why many chargers have a plastic top pin

Pick up a phone charger and you may notice the top pin is plastic, not metal. This is normal on many small chargers. They are built with double insulation, so they do not need an earth connection. The plastic pin is there to open the socket's shutters and hold the plug in place.

A plastic top pin is not, by itself, a sign of a fake. What matters is the build quality, the labelling and where you bought it.

# The voltage question

Chargers for phones are almost always made for a wide range of mains voltages. Look at the **Input** line on the label: something like 100-240V ~50/60Hz means the charger can handle mains in most countries, including Uganda.

If a device's input line shows only one narrow voltage, check that it suits local mains before you use it. When in doubt, ask.

# Avoid forcing two-pin plugs

Chargers bought abroad often come with a two-pin plug. People sometimes push one pin into the earth hole with a pen or a stick to open the shutters, then push the two pins in. Please do not do this.

- It defeats the safety shutters.
- The two-pin plug may not grip the contacts, causing heat and sparking.
- It puts your fingers close to live parts.

If you must use a foreign charger, use a proper travel adapter that is made for Type G sockets. Better still, use a charger with a UK plug.

# Adapters and extension sockets

- **Use proper extension sockets**, ideally with a switch and surge protection, rather than chains of adapters.
- **Do not stack adapters** on top of each other. Each joint adds resistance and a place for heat to build.
- **Do not overload.** Heaters, kettles and irons draw far more than phone chargers. Keep high-power appliances off the same extension as your chargers if you can.
- **Watch for warning signs:** a plug that feels hot, a burning smell, scorch marks on a socket or a crackling sound. Switch off and stop using that socket or extension until it is checked.

# The fuse in the plug

A standard UK-style plug on a cable usually has a small fuse inside. Many phone chargers have the plug built into the body, so there is no fuse you can change. Protection is built into the charger instead. If a charger stops working, replace it rather than opening it.
